Environmental Justice Campaign Writing Guide for LLMs
Comprehensive Style Guide for Petrochemical Pollution and Fenceline Community Campaigns
This guide provides specific instructions, vocabulary, framing strategies, and examples for writing compelling environmental justice campaigns focused on petrochemical facilities and fenceline communities. All recommendations are based on analysis of successful campaigns and communications research.

SECTION 1: CORE FRAMING PRINCIPLES
The Golden Rule: Make the Invisible Visible
Petrochemical pollution is largely invisible (airborne chemicals, parts-per-billion concentrations). Your primary task is to make it concrete, immediate, and visceral.
ALWAYS DO:
Translate abstract data into physical sensations and visual imagery
Use specific chemical names with immediate health effects
Connect to bodily experiences (breathing, skin contact, smell)
Provide concrete comparisons people can visualize
Name specific companies, facilities, and locations
NEVER DO:
Use vague terms like "pollutants" or "contaminants" without specifics
Rely on statistics alone without human stories
Use technical jargon without immediate translation
Make claims about future/distant impacts without present urgency
Use passive voice that obscures responsibility
The Sensory Imperative: If They Can't Feel It, They Won't Act
Petrochemical pollution operates at the threshold of human perception—parts per billion, invisible gases, delayed health effects. Your job is to translate this into sensory experience through concrete details OR sensory metaphor.
THE RULE: Every Key Point Needs Sensory Appeal
Appeal to the five senses OR use metaphor that creates sensory imagery:
SENSORY CONCRETE (Literal physical experience):
"The smell burns the inside of your nose"
"Children wheeze loud enough to hear across the room"
"Orange smoke you can see from the playground"
"Chemical taste that coats your tongue during flares"
"Skin rashes that weep and sting"
SENSORY METAPHOR (Abstract made visceral):
"Living in a chemical siege" (creates feeling of being trapped, surrounded)
"Breathing someone else's profit" (makes economic extraction physical)
"A slow-motion industrial massacre" (conveys scale and violence)
"The plant's shadow falls over every playground" (makes danger visible)
"Sacrifice zone" (implies altar, blood, religious violence)
AVOID: Abstract without sensory anchor:
❌ "The community experiences health impacts from pollution"
❌ "Residents are concerned about emissions"
❌ "There are elevated cancer rates in the area"
TRANSFORM: Add sensory details OR powerful metaphor:
Option 1 (Concrete sensory): "Parents hear their children wheezing at night. Seven-year-olds use inhalers before playing outside. Families smell chemicals strong enough to taste."
Option 2 (Abstract + metaphor): "These communities have become industrial dumping grounds—places where corporations dispose of their poison the same way you'd take out trash, except the trash is benzene and the bin is a child's lungs."
Option 3 (Mixed): "Cancer has colonized this street—12 neighbors dead in five years, tumors in lungs, liver, brain. The plant's emissions are an occupying army that never leaves."
THE SENSORY-ABSTRACT PAIRING FORMULA:
When using abstract concepts, immediately anchor with:
Physical sensation, OR
Vivid metaphor, OR
Specific countable detail
Examples:
"Environmental racism" (abstract) → "the wrong complexion for protection" (metaphor that implies visibility, judgment, denial)
"Community suffering" (abstract) → "parents who check air monitors before letting kids outside, like checking for storms" (concrete behavior + relatable metaphor)
"Systemic injustice" (abstract) → "Shell executives wouldn't let their own children breathe this air—they know exactly what they're doing" (makes power dynamic visceral through contrast)
TEST YOUR WRITING:
For each key sentence, ask:
Can someone smell, see, hear, taste, or touch what I'm describing? (literal sensory)
Does my metaphor create a physical or emotional sensation? (figurative sensory)
Would a reader nod because they can feel what I mean?
If no to all three: rewrite with sensory language.
The Victim-Villain-Victory Framework
Every campaign narrative needs three clear elements:
VICTIMS (Lead with these):
Children and pregnant women (most sympathetic, scientifically vulnerable)
Long-time residents with generational ties to place
People with documented health conditions linked to exposure
Communities that were there BEFORE the facilities (displacement narrative)
VILLAINS (Name them specifically):
Specific companies (Shell, Exxon, Dow, Formosa, Denka, etc.)
Named executives and their compensation
Regulatory agencies that failed to protect
Politicians who accepted campaign donations from polluters
VICTORY (Show pathway forward):
Previous successful campaigns (Love Canal, Warren County, Flint)
Specific policy demands with precedent
Community organizing wins (permits denied, facilities closed)
Regulatory tools that exist but aren't being used

Tier 1: Primary Power Words (Use These Liberally)
Health and Body Terms:
Poison/poisoned/poisoning (direct, visceral)
Cancer (immediate fear response)
Asthma (common, visible, children affected)
Birth defects (morally outrageous)
Neurological damage (affects children's development)
Respiratory disease (connects to breathing)
Chemical exposure (clinical but clear)
Proximity and Invasion Terms:
Fenceline (geographic specificity)
Too close (implies violation of safety)
Breathing distance (emphasizes involuntary exposure)
Surrounded (creates siege imagery)
Neighboring (unwanted proximity)
Living shadow of (plant/refinery/facility)
Injustice and Rights Terms:
Sacrifice zones (mainstream breakthrough term)
Environmental racism (name it explicitly)
Stolen (health/breath/future stolen)
Forced/involuntary (non-consensual exposure)
Dumping (deliberate action on powerless)
Expendable (communities treated as disposable)

Protect (positive framing - what we're FOR)

Demand (not asking, requiring)
Stop (clear, achievable goal)
Prevent (proactive, not just reactive)

For Facilities:

"Over 200 petrochemical facilities concentrated in"
"Refineries operating 24/7 within [distance] of homes"
"Chemical plants older than the safety regulations meant to control them"

For Communities:

For Corporate Behavior:
"Companies that report billions in profits while communities suffer"
"Executives who wouldn't live within 50 miles of their own facilities"
"Corporations that spent more on lobbying than on safety upgrades"
"Plants that routinely exceed permitted emission levels"
"Facilities that delay repairs until after inspection deadlines"
Tier 3: Avoid These Terms (Why They Fail)
NEVER USE:
"Fenceline communities" (bureaucratic, no emotional resonance)
"Environmental justice communities" (academic, distancing)
"Disadvantaged communities" (patronizing, removes agency)
"Vulnerable populations" (passive, victimizing)
"Stakeholders" (corporate language that equalizes power)
"Elevated levels" (too clinical, abstracts harm)
"Cumulative impacts" (technical jargon without explanation)
"Exposure pathways" (bureaucratic, opaque)
REPLACE WITH:
"Families forced to breathe poison" (instead of fenceline communities)
"Residents fighting for survival" (instead of EJ communities)
"Communities under chemical siege" (instead of disadvantaged)
"People protecting their children" (instead of vulnerable populations)
"Residents versus polluters" (instead of stakeholders)
"Benzene at triple the safe limit" (instead of elevated levels)
"Poison from multiple sources" (instead of cumulative impacts)
"How chemicals get into bodies" (instead of exposure pathways)
Tier 4: The Sensory-Metaphor Arsenal
These metaphors transform abstract concepts into visceral, sensory experiences. Use them to make invisible harm visible and felt.
SIEGE AND INVASION METAPHORS (Creates feeling of being trapped, attacked)
"Chemical siege"
"Industrial occupation"
"Toxic invasion"
"Under assault from all sides"
"Living behind enemy lines"
"The plant's shadow falls over every home"
Usage: "It's not just Shell. Mossville is under chemical siege: Dow to the south, Shell to the north, Rubicon to the west. There's no escape route."
SACRIFICE AND TRANSACTION METAPHORS (Makes exploitation explicit and visceral)
"Sacrifice zones"
"Human collateral"
"Industrial dumping ground"
"Trading lives for profits"
"Children's lungs as corporate assets"
"Blood money" (referring to profits)
"Extracting life itself"
Usage: "St. James Parish has become a sacrifice zone—a place where Black children's lungs are the price of white shareholders' dividends."
THEFT AND VIOLATION METAPHORS (Creates sense of something stolen, rights violated)
"Stolen breath"
"Robbed of health"
"Air they never consented to breathe"
"Forced to inhale profit"
"Breathing someone else's waste"
"Trespassing into lungs"
Usage: "Every breath is a violation—Denka's chloroprene trespassing into children's lungs without consent, without compensation, without consequence."
EROSION AND DESTRUCTION METAPHORS (Shows gradual, systemic harm)
"Slow-motion industrial massacre"
"Cancer creeping through the community"
"Chemical erosion of health"
"Breathing poison drop by drop"
"Death by a thousand flares"
"Dissolving from the inside"
Usage: "Cancer is colonizing this street—not with armies but with benzene, not overnight but one lung at a time, until 12 neighbors are gone in five years."
CONTAMINATION AND INFECTION METAPHORS (Triggers disgust and urgency)
"Poison pumped over playgrounds"
"Toxic injection into the air"
"Chemical contamination spreading"
"Industrial infection"
"Polluting the bloodstream of the community"
Usage: "Shell doesn't just emit chemicals—it injects poison into the air these families breathe, contaminating lungs the way a virus contaminates blood."
VISIBILITY AND WITNESSING METAPHORS (Forces attention, breaks invisibility)
"Making the invisible visible"
"What executives refuse to see"
"The pollution you can smell but they can't measure"
"Hidden in plain sight"
"What cameras can't capture but bodies record"
Usage: "The EPA's monitors don't capture the smell that wakes families at 3 AM, the taste that coats tongues during flares, the stinging that makes eyes water. But residents' bodies record what instruments miss."
COLONIZATION AND DISPLACEMENT METAPHORS (Connects to historical patterns)
"Industrial colonization"
"Corporate land grab"
"Slow erasure"
"Ethnic cleansing by emission"
"Gentrification by poison"
Usage: "This isn't development—it's industrial colonization. Mossville existed for 200 years before petrochemical companies arrived. Now the community is nearly gone, erased not by force but by poison, one buyout at a time."
FUTURE THEFT METAPHORS (Makes long-term impact immediate)
"Stealing tomorrow"
"Children born pre-poisoned"
"Mortgaging lives for quarterly profits"
"Future foreclosed before birth"
Usage: "Shell isn't just poisoning today's children—it's stealing the health of children not yet born, pumping cancer risk into the air that pregnant mothers breathe."
HOW TO USE METAPHORS EFFECTIVELY:
Introduce, then ground: Lead with metaphor, follow with concrete detail


"Mossville is under chemical siege. Shell to the north flares benzene over homes. Dow to the south releases 1,3-butadiene. Residents can't escape."
Layer abstract-metaphor-concrete:


"Environmental racism (abstract) is the wrong complexion for protection (metaphor)—when white suburbs get EPA response in weeks but Black children breathe benzene for decades (concrete)."
Return to metaphor as anchor: Use same metaphor throughout piece to create coherence


Open with "sacrifice zone," return to it in conclusion
Test metaphor power: Does it create physical sensation or emotion? Can readers feel surrounded (siege), violated (theft), revolted (contamination)?


AVOID:
Mixed metaphors ("chemical siege that's drowning the community")
Cliché without punch ("tip of the iceberg," "canary in the coal mine")
Metaphors that minimize harm ("David vs. Goliath" makes it seem like a fair fight)
Metaphors without follow-up concrete detail
REMEMBER: Metaphor amplifies message but doesn't replace concrete detail. Use both.

SECTION 4: THE SUCCESs FORMULA FOR EVERY MESSAGE
Every campaign message should be evaluated against these six criteria (Chip & Dan Heath's "Made to Stick"). If a message fails more than two criteria, revise it.
1. SIMPLE (One Core Message)
DO: "Every child deserves to breathe clean air. Shell's refinery is poisoning ours."
DON'T: "The cumulative impacts of petrochemical facility emissions, when considered in the context of historical exposure pathways and compounding socioeconomic vulnerabilities, create disproportionate health burdens for environmental justice communities in the industrial corridor."
Rule: Strip to single profound truth. If you can't say it in one sentence, it's not simple enough.
2. UNEXPECTED (Break Patterns, Create Curiosity)
DO: "Shell executives wouldn't let their own children play in yards like ours. Why should we?" "Louisiana is the richest oil state and has the poorest health outcomes. That's not a coincidence."
DON'T: "Industrial facilities have been shown to have negative health impacts."
Rule: Use counter-intuitive contrasts. Shock people with facts that violate expectations. Create curiosity gaps that demand answers.
3. CONCRETE (Sensory, Human-Scale, Specific)
Make ideas tangible through sensory details or sensory metaphors. The brain processes sensory information faster and remembers it longer than abstract concepts.
PATH 1: LITERAL SENSORY DETAILS
Ground abstractions in physical, observable reality:
DO:
"LaTrisha's daughter uses her inhaler three times before school. The Denka plant is 400 yards from her house."
"When the plant flares, the smell is so strong you can taste it in your mouth."
"Dr. Mona measured lead levels of 5 micrograms per deciliter in Flint children—double what triggers intervention."
DON'T:
"Studies indicate elevated respiratory disease prevalence."
"Pollution levels exceed regulatory thresholds."
PATH 2: SENSORY METAPHOR
When the harm is invisible or complex, use metaphor that creates physical sensation:
DO:
"These neighborhoods are sacrifice zones—places where children's lungs are the altar and corporate profit is the god."
"Living here is like being a non-smoker in a bar where everyone smokes, except the cigarettes are refineries and you can never leave."
"The plant's emissions are a slow flood—you can't see it rising, but one day you look around and half your neighbors have drowned in cancer."
DON'T:
"The cumulative burden of multiple facilities creates disproportionate impacts."
"Systemic factors contribute to adverse outcomes."
THE TWO-LAYER TECHNIQUE:
Pair abstract concepts with BOTH concrete detail AND metaphor for maximum impact:
"Environmental racism isn't just a concept—it's the smell of chemicals burning your daughter's throat while she plays in her own backyard. It's Shell executives who wouldn't live within 50 miles of their own facilities. It's a government that responds to benzene in white suburbs within weeks but lets Black children breathe it for decades. That's what 'the wrong complexion for protection' means in practice."
Analysis:
Abstract term: "Environmental racism"
Concrete detail: "smell of chemicals burning your daughter's throat"
Metaphor: "wrong complexion for protection"
Observable contrast: "weeks vs. decades"
SENSORY LANGUAGE HIERARCHY:
Most Powerful → Least Powerful
Direct physical sensation ("burns your throat," "makes you gag")
Observable action ("using inhaler three times," "running inside")
Countable/measurable ("12 funerals," "400 yards," "three times EPA limit")
Vivid metaphor ("chemical siege," "sacrifice zone," "industrial dumping ground")
Descriptive detail ("orange smoke," "sweet chemical smell")
Abstract with context ("cancer rates 50% higher than state average")
Pure abstraction ("health impacts," "environmental concerns")
Aim for levels 1-4 in every key message. Use level 5-6 for supporting detail. Avoid level 7 unless immediately paired with 1-4.
RULE: If readers can't visualize it, feel it in their body, or see it in their mind through metaphor—rewrite it.
4. CREDIBLE (Testable, Authoritative, Human-Scale)
DO: "Tulane researchers found cancer rates 50% higher within two miles of these facilities." "EPA's own data shows Denka's Reserve plant creates the highest cancer risk in America." "Ask anyone on Diamond Street how many neighbors died of cancer. They'll count on both hands."
DON'T: "Experts agree pollution is harmful." "Many people have experienced health issues."
Rule: Cite specific studies, name researchers, use government data, or provide testable claims ("go count the cancer cases yourself"). Make skeptics able to verify.
5. EMOTIONAL (Make People Feel for People)
DO: "Sharon Lavigne's neighbors died one by one. Now she's fighting to make sure no more children lose parents to preventable cancers." "Michael's son was born with a cleft palate. Three other babies on his street had birth defects. The Dow plant has been there for 30 years."
DON'T: "Statistical analysis reveals correlation between proximity and adverse health outcomes."
Rule: Tell individual stories. Name people. Describe specific losses. Show what was stolen. Make it personal and particular, never abstract. People feel for people, not percentages.
6. STORY (Provide Mental Simulation)
DO: Challenge Plot: "For five years, Formosa thought they could build a $9 billion complex in St. James Parish. But Sharon Lavigne and RISE St. James organized. They testified. They prayed. They fought. In 2022, Formosa withdrew. If they did it, so can we."
Connection Plot: "Robert Taylor is white. Sharon Lavigne is Black. Mossville residents are Creole. They don't agree on everything. But they all agree: no child should breathe poison. That's why they stand together."
Creativity Plot: "Residents couldn't get EPA to test their air. So Bucket Brigades gave them tools to do it themselves. Now communities have their own data—data agencies can't ignore."
DON'T: "Community organizing has proven effective in various contexts."
Rule: Use one of three plots: Challenge (underdog wins), Connection (bridging divides), Creativity (innovative solution). Stories provide simulation (how to act) and inspiration (motivation to act).

SECTION 5: STRATEGIC FRAMING FORMULAS
The Secondhand Smoke Frame
Template: "[Community] is breathing [specific chemical] from [company]'s [facility]. They didn't choose this exposure. They can't avoid it. It's secondhand pollution—someone else's profit poisoning their lungs."
Why This Works:
Emphasizes involuntary nature
Creates clear victim (innocent) and villain (polluter)
Activates moral foundations around fairness and harm
Provides precedent (secondhand smoke bans succeeded)
Example Applications: "Residents of Reserve, LA are breathing chloroprene from Denka's plant. They can't not breathe. It's secondhand pollution—Denka's profits poisoning their children."
The Wrong Complexion for Protection Frame
Template: "When [affluent/white area] had [similar problem], action came within [timeframe]. But [fenceline community], which is [X]% [Black/Latino/low-income], has suffered [chemical/facility] for [years] while officials look the other way. This is environmental racism."
Why This Works:
Makes systemic racism explicit and undeniable
Uses comparison to create moral outrage
Provides concrete evidence of disparate treatment
Refuses euphemism (names racism directly)
Example Applications: "When benzene levels rose in affluent Baton Rouge suburbs, EPA responded within weeks. But in predominantly Black neighborhoods along the industrial corridor, residents have breathed benzene for decades while agencies issue violations without enforcement. This is environmental racism, pure and simple."
The Sacrifice Zone Transaction Frame
Template: "[Company] makes [profit amount] while [community] suffers [health impact]. [Community] has been sacrificed so [distant beneficiary] can [benefit]. This transaction is unacceptable."
Why This Works:
Makes exchange explicit (lives for profits)
Names who benefits and who pays
Creates moral clarity about injustice
Implies solution (end the sacrifice)
Example Applications: "ExxonMobil reported $56 billion in profits last year while children in North Baton Rouge miss school because they can't breathe. These neighborhoods have been sacrificed so distant shareholders can profit. This transaction must end."
The Compound Harm Frame
Template: "It's not just [one facility]. [Community] is surrounded: [Company A] to the north, [Company B] to the south, [Company C] across the street. Each plant alone violates limits. Together, they create a chemical siege."
Why This Works:
Makes cumulative impacts visual and concrete
Uses military metaphor (siege) to convey severity
Shows intentional pattern (not random placement)
Explains why single-facility regulations fail
Example Applications: "It's not just Shell. Mossville is surrounded: Shell Norco to the north, Dow to the south, Rubicon to the west. Each plant exceeds emissions limits. Together, they've turned a town into a toxic trap."
The Children's Future Frame
Template: "Every parent wants their child to [universal aspiration]. But in [community], children [specific harm] because of [company/facility]. These kids didn't choose to be born next to a refinery. They deserve the same future as [affluent comparison]."
Why This Works:
Children are most sympathetic victims
Appeals to universal parental values
Creates stark injustice (innocent children harmed)
Crosses political divides (everyone cares about kids)
Example Applications: "Every parent wants their child to run and play without gasping for air. But in Reserve, children use inhalers at three times the national average because Denka's plant pumps chloroprene over their playgrounds. These kids didn't choose to be born in a cancer hot zone. They deserve the same healthy childhood as children in Metairie or Baton Rouge suburbs."
The Sensory Contrast Frame
Juxtapose what should be (sensory normalcy) with what is (sensory violation) to create moral outrage through felt experience.
TEMPLATE: "In [normal place], [sensory experience of safety/normalcy]. But in [fenceline community], [sensory experience of violation]—because [company] [action]."
WHY THIS WORKS:
Creates immediate empathy through universal sensory experiences
Makes injustice visceral (what you take for granted, they're denied)
Uses contrast to amplify both the normalcy and the violation
Triggers mirror neurons (readers imagine experiencing both scenarios)
SENSORY CONTRAST CATEGORIES:
BREATHING/AIR CONTRASTS:
"In most neighborhoods, children run outside and breathe deeply after rain—that clean, fresh smell. In Reserve, children hold their breath when they go outside during flares—the chemical smell burns their nose and throat. Denka's plant is why one community gets fresh air and another gets chloroprene."
"Suburban parents worry about pollen counts. Fenceline parents check benzene monitors before opening windows—will today's reading be three times EPA's safe limit or five?"
SOUND/PEACE CONTRASTS:
"Most families hear crickets at night, maybe distant traffic. Reserve families hear industrial hums, sudden flares, explosion-like sounds at 2 AM that shake walls. Shell's refinery never sleeps, so neither do the families within earshot."
SIGHT/LANDSCAPE CONTRASTS:
"Drive through Metairie and you see trees, playground equipment, gardens. Drive through Mossville and you see flare stacks taller than houses, orange flames lighting bedroom windows at night, industrial pipes where pecan groves used to be. Shell built profit by erasing a landscape."
SMELL/SENSORY SAFETY CONTRASTS:
"In your neighborhood, opening windows on spring days means smelling flowers, cut grass, rain. In St. James Parish, opening windows means gambling—will you smell something sweet and chemical that makes you nauseous? Will the taste coat your tongue? Formosa wanted to make this worse."
TOUCH/BODILY AUTONOMY CONTRASTS:
"Most people's skin doesn't burn from their own yard air. Most children don't get rashes from playing outside. But fenceline kids come inside with itching, redness, reactions to air that shouldn't harm. Their bodies become test sites for industrial emissions."
ACTIVITY/FREEDOM CONTRASTS:
"Affluent kids ride bikes until streetlights come on. Fenceline kids stay inside when monitors spike—bikes unused, childhood on hold, freedom stolen by someone else's profit margin."
TEMPORAL CONTRASTS (Before/After):
"Mossville elders remember sleeping with windows open, summer breeze cooling the house. Now windows stay sealed year-round, AC running constantly, trying to filter out what petrochemical companies pump in. That's not progress—that's theft of basic comfort."
THE DOUBLE-SENSORY TECHNIQUE:
Layer two sensory contrasts for maximum impact:
"In Baton Rouge suburbs, kids play outside until dark, parents hear them laughing, smell barbecue from neighbor's grill. In North Baton Rouge fenceline neighborhoods, kids stay inside when the smell gets strong, parents hear them coughing, taste chemicals that drift from ExxonMobil's refinery. Same city. Same sky. Different protections. That's the geography of racism."
ADVANCED: THE EXECUTIVE CONTRAST
Compare what corporate executives experience with what communities experience:
"Shell executives breathe filtered office air, live in neighborhoods with strict zoning, send their kids to schools miles from any refinery. Meanwhile, Norco residents breathe whatever Shell releases, live in the plant's shadow, send their kids to schools where the playground sits within sight of flare stacks. Shell knows exactly what safe distance looks like—they just don't provide it to everyone."
USAGE GUIDELINES:
Start with universal experience readers can relate to (fresh air, kids playing, peaceful sleep)
Contrast with fenceline reality using specific sensory details
Name the company/system responsible for the difference
Keep contrast tight (2-3 sentences maximum per side)
AVOID:
False equivalence (don't say "both have challenges")
Minimizing normal privilege ("not everyone has it easy")
Comparison without sensory detail (staying abstract on both sides)
TEST:
Can readers imagine experiencing both scenarios?
Does the contrast create discomfort/anger?
Is the injustice immediately clear?
If yes to all three, the sensory contrast is working.
The Before/After Displacement Frame
Template: "[Community] was here first. [Families] built [description] generations before [company] arrived. Now [company] wants them gone. This isn't development—it's displacement."
Why This Works:
Establishes moral prior claim (we were here first)
Reverses narrative (plants invaded, not residents intruding)
Connects to displacement/gentrification concerns
Emphasizes community history and roots
Example Applications: "Mossville was founded in 1790 by free people of color. Families grew pecans and raised children for generations before Shell built refineries in the 1950s. Now Shell has bought out most residents. This isn't economic development—it's the erasure of a historic Black community."

SECTION 6: RESEARCH AND DATA INTEGRATION
How to Use Statistics Effectively
ALWAYS Pair Numbers with Human Stories:
WEAK: "Cancer rates are elevated in the corridor."
STRONG: "Sharon Lavigne's street in Welcome, LA has lost 12 people to cancer in a one-block radius. Tulane researchers found cancer rates in St. James Parish are 50% higher than Louisiana's average—and Louisiana already has the second-highest cancer death rate in the nation."
Formula: Personal story → Supporting data → Context that shows severity
Key Statistics to Include (With Sources)
Louisiana Cancer Alley Facts:
"85-mile stretch along Mississippi River between Baton Rouge and New Orleans"
"Over 200 petrochemical facilities concentrated in seven parishes"
"Cancer rates up to 50% higher than state average in some areas"
"Louisiana has 2nd highest cancer death rate in the U.S."
"Residents in this corridor face cancer risk from air toxics as high as 50 times the national average"
Denka Reserve Facility:
"Highest census tract cancer risk in America (EPA data)"
"Emits chloroprene at levels 15 times EPA's safe limit"
"800 times more likely to develop cancer than if they lived elsewhere (EPA estimate)"
National Fenceline Context:
"More than 40% of Americans—135 million people—live within 3 miles of high-risk chemical facilities (EPA)"
"Communities within one mile of petrochemical facilities have 66% higher cancer risk"
"Black Americans are 75% more likely to live in fenceline communities"
Economic Injustice:
"ExxonMobil made $56 billion profit in 2022"
"Shell reported $40 billion profit in 2022"
"Average household income in fenceline communities: $35,000"
"CEO compensation packages: $20-30 million annually"
How to Use Scientific Studies
Template for Citing Research: "[University/Institution] researchers found [specific finding]. According to [Lead Researcher Name], '[quote that's in plain language].' The study, published in [Journal], examined [specific population/area]."
Example: "Tulane University researchers found children living within two miles of Louisiana petrochemical facilities have significantly elevated asthma rates. Community Science and Data Justice
Frame Community-Collected Data as Equally Valid:

SECTION 9: WHAT TO AVOID
Language That Undermines Your Message
SENSORY-FREE ABSTRACTION
Abstract language is not inherently weak—but abstraction without sensory anchor is. Never use abstract concepts without pairing them with concrete details OR powerful metaphor.
THE PROBLEM: Abstract concepts float away from readers' minds. Sensory language sticks.
WEAK: Abstraction alone ❌ "The community experiences health impacts" ❌ "Residents are concerned about pollution" ❌ "There are elevated cancer rates" ❌ "Environmental conditions create challenges" ❌ "People suffer from exposure"
BETTER: Abstract + Concrete Detail ✓ "The community experiences health impacts—children using inhalers three times daily, parents with chronic nosebleeds, seven cancer diagnoses on one block" ✓ "Residents are concerned about pollution—they smell chemicals strong enough to gag, see orange smoke over playgrounds, taste metal in the air during flares"
BEST: Abstract + Metaphor + Concrete ✓ "These neighborhoods have become sacrifice zones (metaphor)—places where corporate profit requires human collateral. Children use inhalers before playing outside. Parents develop rare cancers at 50. Families smell chemicals that burn their throats (concrete details). Shell made $40 billion last year while 12 neighbors on one street died of cancer (concrete scale/contrast)."
THE TRANSFORMATION PATTERNS:
Pattern 1: Replace abstract nouns with sensory verbs
Abstract: "health impacts" → Sensory: "wheezing," "coughing," "bleeding"
Abstract: "pollution exposure" → Sensory: "breathing benzene," "skin burning," "eyes watering"
Abstract: "community suffering" → Sensory: "parents checking monitors," "children staying indoors," "families packing to leave"
Pattern 2: Pair abstract concept with immediate metaphor
"Environmental racism—the wrong complexion for protection"
"Cumulative impacts—death by a thousand flares"
"Systemic injustice—a government that protects white lungs faster than Black lungs"
Pattern 3: Follow abstraction with "looks like/sounds like/feels like"
"Cancer rates are elevated. That looks like 12 funerals on one block in five years."
"Residents experience exposure. That sounds like children wheezing loud enough to hear across the room."
"The community faces health challenges. That feels like burning in your throat when you breathe your own yard air."
THE 3-SECOND RULE:
Reader should encounter sensory language within 3 seconds (roughly 10-15 words) of any abstract term.
Too slow: "The environmental justice implications of petrochemical facility siting decisions create disproportionate health outcome disparities in communities of color, particularly in Louisiana's industrial corridor where residents face elevated exposure to carcinogenic compounds." (42 words before anything sensory/concrete)
Fast enough: "Environmental racism in Louisiana means Black children breathing chloroprene—a cancer-causing chemical—at levels 15 times what EPA calls safe." (Sensory language "breathing chloroprene" arrives in 6 words; concrete number "15 times" in 15 words)
SPECIFIC ABSTRACTIONS TO NEVER USE ALONE:
Always pair these with sensory details or metaphor:
❌ "Health impacts" → ✓ Add: what body part? what sensation? what visible sign? ❌ "Environmental concerns" → ✓ Add: what can you smell/see/taste? ❌ "Community suffering" → ✓ Add: what specific action shows this? ❌ "Pollution exposure" → ✓ Add: which chemical? through what route? causing what feeling? ❌ "Quality of life" → ✓ Add: what specific activity is now impossible? ❌ "Systemic issues" → ✓ Add: what contrast reveals the system? what pattern repeats?
REMEMBER:
Abstract concepts are useful for framing big ideas
But they must be anchored with sensory language within seconds
Use concrete details (physical, observable) OR powerful metaphor (creates sensation)
Test: Would a reader feel this in their body or see it in their mind? If no, add sensory language.
PASSIVE VOICE: ❌ "The community has been impacted by emissions" ✓ "Shell's refinery poisoned the community"
❌ "Chemicals were released" ✓ "Dow dumped benzene over residential neighborhoods"
Rule: Name the actor. Someone made a decision. Use active voice.
EUPHEMISM: ❌ "Environmental justice communities" ✓ "Communities poisoned by industry"
❌ "Disadvantaged populations" ✓ "Families forced to breathe toxic air"
❌ "Emissions events" ✓ "Chemical accidents" or "toxic releases"
Rule: If industry uses the term, don't. Use plain language that names harm.
FALSE BALANCE: ❌ "Industry stakeholders and community stakeholders need to find common ground" ✓ "Companies must stop poisoning communities. There's no middle ground between clean air and cancer."
❌ "Both sides have valid concerns" ✓ "Residents have a right to breathe. Companies have violated that right."
Rule: This isn't a debate between equals. Don't equalize power.
VICTIM LANGUAGE WITHOUT AGENCY: ❌ "These vulnerable communities need help" ✓ "These communities are organizing to shut down the facilities poisoning them"
❌ "Residents are suffering" ✓ "Residents are fighting back"
Rule: Show people as agents of change, not passive victims.
FUTURE-FOCUSED FEAR WITHOUT PRESENT URGENCY: ❌ "Climate change will impact these areas" ✓ "Children are using inhalers today because of emissions from facilities that also drive climate chaos"
❌ "Future generations may suffer" ✓ "Families are suffering now. Every day we wait, more people get sick."
Rule: Present tense. Immediate harm. Name people affected today.
JARGON AND ABSTRACTION: ❌ "Cumulative impacts create disproportionate health burdens" ✓ "Poison from multiple plants is making families sick at higher rates than anywhere else"
❌ "Exposure pathways include air, water, and soil media" ✓ "Chemicals get into bodies through breathing contaminated air, drinking poisoned water, and children playing in toxic soil"
Rule: If a non-expert wouldn't understand it immediately, rewrite it.
Topics and Frames to Avoid
DON'T Lead With:
Technical regulatory details
Long historical background
Scientific uncertainty ("studies suggest," "research indicates")
Economic trade-offs ("jobs vs. environment")
Distant/abstract impacts
Policy wonk discussions
DO Lead With:
Personal stories of harm
Children's health
Named companies causing harm
Clear injustice
Immediate health threats
Community organizing and resistance
DON'T Accept:
"Job creation" justification (counter: "You can't spend a paycheck if you're dead")
"Economic development" framing (counter: "This is economic exploitation")
"Best available technology" defense (counter: "The best technology is not building the facility at all")
"Voluntary" buyout programs (counter: "It's not voluntary when you're being poisoned")
"Community benefits" bribes (counter: "No amount of money compensates for stolen health")

Translating Visual Evidence into Sensory Language
Photos and data visualizations capture what eyes can see. Your job is to translate them into multi-sensory experiences that activate all five senses—making readers feel present in the moment.
THE PRINCIPLE: Extend Beyond the Frame
What the camera captures is only one sense (sight). Add the senses the camera cannot record.
PHOTO: Industrial facility at night, flare burning
WEAK caption (sight only): "Shell's Norco refinery flaring at night, December 2023."
STRONG caption (multi-sensory): "Shell's Norco refinery flares at night, visible from bedroom windows. Residents report the smell is strong enough to taste, with a sharp chemical sweetness that coats the tongue. The sound—a deep industrial roar—makes sleep difficult for families within half a mile. December 2023."
Analysis: Added smell (taste, chemical sweetness), sound (roar), touch (coating sensation), and human impact (sleep disruption)
PHOTO: Child with inhaler
WEAK caption (sight only): "Local child with asthma inhaler, Reserve, LA."
STRONG caption (multi-sensory + context): "Seven-year-old Jayden uses his rescue inhaler before playing outside—checking air quality first has become routine. 'Sometimes the air smells weird and makes your chest feel tight,' he says. His mother reports he uses the inhaler 3-4 times daily, far more than the doctor recommended. Reserve, LA, 2024. Denka's chloroprene plant is 400 yards from his house."
Analysis: Added sensation (tight chest), smell (weird), sound (child's voice), behavioral detail (checking air quality), quantifiable data (3-4 times daily), and geographic specificity (400 yards)
DATA VISUALIZATION: Map showing facility locations
WEAK description (abstract): "Map showing concentration of petrochemical facilities in Louisiana's industrial corridor."
STRONG description (sensory implication): "Look at this map of Louisiana's Cancer Alley: over 200 facilities clustered along 85 miles. Now imagine living inside one of those red dots—facilities so close you see flare stacks from your porch, hear industrial sounds 24/7, smell chemicals when wind shifts. Each dot represents someone's neighborhood, someone's children breathing what those facilities release."
Analysis: Transforms abstract map into lived experience through sight (flare stacks), sound (24/7 industrial noise), smell (chemicals), and perspective shift (imagine being there)
DATA VISUALIZATION: Bar graph of cancer rates
WEAK description (numbers only): "Cancer rates in St. James Parish are 50% higher than Louisiana average."
STRONG description (human scale + sensory): "This graph shows cancer rates 50% higher in St. James Parish. That statistic has a smell—the chemical odor residents report during flares. It has a sound—the coughing that wakes parents at night. It has a face—Sharon Lavigne can point to 12 houses on her street where neighbors died of cancer. The red bar on this chart represents people who couldn't breathe, couldn't stop tumors from growing, couldn't escape the emissions they were forced to inhale."
Analysis: Anchors data in sensory experience (smell, sound) and human reality (pointing to houses, inability to breathe)

WEAK description (technical): "Benzene levels exceeded EPA standards 47 times in 2023."
STRONG description (sensory + implication): "These spikes—47 times in 2023—represent moments when families rushed inside, closed windows despite summer heat, smelled something sweet and wrong in the air. THE SENSORY EXPANSION FORMULA:
For any visual evidence, systematically add:
What it sounds like there (industrial hums, kids coughing, warning sirens, silence where birds should sing)
What it smells like (chemical sweetness, rotten eggs, metallic, burning)
What it feels like (burning throat, tight chest, stinging eyes, nausea)
What it tastes like (metallic coating, chemical aftertaste, "you can taste it in the air")

WEAK caption (observational): "Mossville, LA in 1960 and 2024. Many houses have been demolished."
STRONG caption (sensory loss narrative): "Mossville in 1960: Tree-lined streets where children played until dark, the smell of pecan trees and home cooking, sounds of families on porches, windows open to summer breezes. Analysis: Transforms visual comparison into multi-sensory loss story
REMEMBER:
Cameras capture sight; writers must add sound, smell, touch, taste
Data captures numbers; writers must add human-scale sensory reality
Maps capture geography; writers must add lived experience of place
Always ask: "What would I sense if I stood in this photo right now?"
The goal is making readers feel present—not just seeing evidence, but experiencing it through all senses.

The Perfect Sentence Structure
FORMULA 1: Sensory Concrete [Named person] + [sensory verb/detail] + because [named company] + [specific action]
Examples:
"Maria's son uses his inhaler three times before breakfast because Shell flares benzene every morning"
"Robert tastes chemicals in his mouth during plant releases because Dow operates without adequate emission controls"
"LaTrisha's daughter vomits from the smell because Denka pumps chloroprene over residential areas"
FORMULA 2: Sensory Metaphor [Community/group] + [metaphor that creates sensation] + [concrete grounding]
Examples:
"Reserve residents are breathing someone else's profit—chloroprene at 15 times EPA's safe limit, 24 hours a day"
"Mossville is under chemical siege—Shell to the north, Dow to the south, emissions from all directions with nowhere to escape"
"St. James Parish has become a sacrifice zone where Black children's lungs are traded for white shareholders' dividends—cancer risk 50% higher than state average"
FORMULA 3: Sensory Contrast [Normal sensory experience] + but [violated sensory experience] + because [company/system]
Examples:
"Most kids smell flowers when playing outside. Reserve kids smell chloroprene that burns their throats. Denka is the difference."
"Suburban parents hear crickets at night. Fenceline parents hear industrial hums and 2 AM explosions that shake walls. ExxonMobil never sleeps."
"In your neighborhood, opening windows means fresh air. In ours, it means gambling whether the chemical smell will make you gag. Shell created that reality."
FORMULA 4: Abstract Anchored [Abstract concept] + [powerful metaphor] + [concrete sensory detail]
Examples:
"Environmental racism is the wrong complexion for protection—EPA responding to white suburbs in weeks but letting Black children breathe benzene for decades"
"Sacrifice zones aren't policy abstractions—they're places where children check air monitors before playing outside, where neighbors count cancer deaths on two hands"
"Cumulative impacts means death by a thousand flares—each plant alone exceeds limits, together they create air that burns your throat and steals your breath"
THE REVISION TEST:
Take any sentence and run it through these questions:
Sensory check: Can readers see, smell, hear, taste, or feel what's described?


If NO → Add sensory detail or sensory metaphor
Specificity check: Are there names (people, companies, places) and numbers?


If NO → Add specific identifiers
Agency check: Is someone doing something to someone else (not passive)?


If NO → Identify actor and action
Impact check: Would this sentence make someone angry, sad, or compelled to act?


If NO → Increase sensory intensity or metaphor power
WEAK SENTENCE TRANSFORMATION EXAMPLE:
Original: "The community experiences health problems from industrial pollution."
Fails: No sensory detail, no names, passive construction, no emotional impact
Revision path:
Add sensory: "The community smells chemicals, children wheeze, parents develop cancers"
Add specifics: "Reserve residents smell chloroprene, children use inhalers, parents develop liver cancer"
Add agency: "Denka's plant pumps chloroprene—residents smell it, children wheeze from it, parents die from it"
Add metaphor: "Denka's plant injects chloroprene into Reserve air—residents smell it burning their throats, children wheeze after breathing it, parents die from cancers it causes. This is industrial poisoning in slow motion."
Final: Hit all four tests. Sensory (smell, burn, wheeze). Specific (Denka, chloroprene, Reserve, cancer). Active (Denka pumps/injects). Impact (slow-motion poisoning).
REMEMBER: Every key sentence should appeal to the senses—either through literal sensory detail OR through metaphor that creates felt experience. If a sentence doesn't make readers see, feel, or imagine, it's not working hard enough.
