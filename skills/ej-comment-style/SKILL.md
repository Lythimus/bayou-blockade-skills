# EJ Comment Style

The register of the public comments that have actually stopped petrochemical projects in
Louisiana: the Tulane Environmental Law Clinic's 1997 Shintech Title V petition, Earthjustice's
2024 Formosa Plastics PM2.5 comment, the 2021 South Louisiana Methanol §404 letter, and the 2022
CP2 LNG coastal-use comment.

**Before drafting anything in this style, Read
`skills/ej-comment-style/references/excerpts.md`.** It holds verbatim passages from all four
letters, labeled by source and technique, and it is what the moves below refer to. Do not draft
from this file's descriptions alone — the cadence is the thing being taught, and it only
transfers from the samples.

---

## SECTION 0: THE ONE THING TO GET RIGHT

**This is not campaign writing. The outrage is carried entirely by cited facts.**

A filed comment has two audiences. The first is the permit writer. The second — the one that
determines how you write — is a judge reading the administrative record two years from now to
decide whether the agency's approval was arbitrary and capricious. Everything in the document
exists to make that second reader's job easy.

Two consequences follow, and they govern every other rule in this file:

1. **Preservation over persuasion.** Under administrative exhaustion, an argument not raised
   with reasonable specificity during the comment period is generally forfeited on appeal. A
   comment that persuades nobody but preserves eleven issues has done its job. A comment that
   moves the room and preserves nothing has failed.
2. **Every sentence must be one the agency has to answer.** Agencies must respond to
   significant comments. A sentence that states a fact with a citation and ties it to a legal
   standard demands a response. A sentence expressing concern does not.

### Relationship to `bayou:toxic-truth-teller-style`

These two skills are complements, and mixing them ruins both.

| | `toxic-truth-teller-style` | `ej-comment-style` (this) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product | Petitions, social posts, press, testimony | The filed document |
| Reader | The public, the press, the room | The permit writer and the reviewing judge |
| Register | Sensory, metaphoric, named villains | Flat declarative, cited, statute-anchored |
| Success | People act | The record holds up on appeal |

**If you find yourself reaching for a metaphor, you want the other skill.** "Chemical siege,"
"sacrifice zone," "stolen breath," "poison" as a verb — all correct in a campaign, all
liabilities in a filing. They give the agency a costless way to classify the submission as
emotional opposition and dispose of it in one line of the response-to-comments document.

The single exception, and how to spend it, is in Section 3.

## SECTION 1: REGISTER

### The core sentence pattern

> **[Agency] must [act], because [applicant] [did or failed to do specific thing]. [Citation].**

Modal verbs are doing the work. Rank them and use the strongest one the law supports:

- **"must"** — a nondiscretionary duty. Use whenever a statute, regulation, or case imposes one.
- **"cannot lawfully"** — the agency lacks the power, not merely the inclination.
- **"should"** — discretionary. Reserve it for genuinely discretionary asks (hold a hearing,
  extend a comment period). Using "should" where "must" is available concedes discretion you
  did not have to concede.
- **"we request" / "we urge"** — for relief that is procedural or courteous. Never for the
  central ask.

Never: "we feel," "we are concerned that," "we would hope," "it seems," "arguably."

### No adjective may carry an argument

If the adjective is doing the work, the sentence has no evidence in it. Replace the adjective
with the number that justified it.

| Instead of | Write |
| --- | --- |
| "massive wetland destruction" | "impacts to 38.9 acres of wetlands and jurisdictional waters" |
| "the plant is dangerously close to homes" | "five 250-foot-diameter methanol storage tanks within one mile of residences and adjacent to Welcome Park" |
| "emissions would be enormous" | "104,520 pounds of vinyl chloride per year — a 75% increase over total statewide releases, confined to one parish" |
| "the analysis was woefully inadequate" | "the analysis modeled 1.2 km from the fenceline and did not assess compliance with the standard currently in force" |

Adjectives that survive are the ones a court has already defined: *arbitrary and capricious*,
*significant*, *practicable*, *cumulative*, *reasonably foreseeable*. Those are terms of art —
use them precisely, in the sense the governing standard gives them, and never as intensifiers.

### Intensity comes from the fact, not from the wrapper

The strongest lines in the corpus are the flattest. Study the cadence:

> "Regardless of Formosa Plastics' assumptions, the outcome of this modeling is clear."

> "There is no evidence for these claims."

> "This is improper segmentation."

> "Venture Global fails those tests."

Short declarative, no adverb, arriving immediately after the evidence that earns it. Build the
paragraph so the fact does the damage, then state the conclusion in six words and stop. Do not
follow it with an explanation of how damaging it was.

### Name the failure as a legal defect, not as bad faith

You may say what the applicant *did* and what the record *shows*. Do not characterize motive.

- Not: "Formosa deliberately concealed its modeling to mislead the agency."
- Yes: "Formosa Plastics did not update its modeling, and LDEQ did not require it to do so."

The second is stronger: it is unrebuttable, it is a defect the agency now owns, and it cannot
be dismissed as an accusation. Where intent genuinely matters (a scrubbed report, a document
filed in the wrong docket), state the documentary sequence and dates and let it speak.

### Voice and person

Institutional first-person plural — "Commenters write to object," "we urge," "we request a
public hearing." Define the group once in the first sentence and use the defined term
throughout. Active voice for the agency's and the applicant's duties, always: name who must do
what. Passive voice is permitted only where the actor is genuinely irrelevant.

Contractions: none. This is the one place this style is stiffer than the rest of the toolkit.

## SECTION 2: DOCUMENT ARCHITECTURE

```
Date
Delivery method (Via E-mail: <address>; Submitted via Internet comments and electronic mail)
Addressee — name, title, office, agency, address
Re: <Applicant> <permit type>, <permit/application/AI numbers>, <docket>
Dear <Name>:

¶1   WHO objects + WHAT they object to + THE NUMBERED ASK
¶2   What is attached (expert report, exhibits) and who prepared it, with credentials
¶3   Reservation of rights and signal of escalation

I.   Factual and Regulatory Background        <- dates, permit history, what changed
II.  Legal Background                          <- the standards you will hold them to
III+ Numbered substantive arguments            <- one defect per heading; heading states the demand
     Conclusion / relief                       <- enumerated, closing forward

Respectfully submitted,
<signature block — attorney, organization, address, email>
Cc: <the agency that supervises this agency>
```

### The heading is the argument

Every substantive heading is a full sentence that states the conclusion the agency must reach.
A reader who skims only the headings must come away with the entire case.

- Not: "IV. Alternatives Analysis"
- Yes: "IV. LDNR Cannot Grant the Permit Because the Application is Not Complete."
- Yes: "II. Formosa Plastics would cause or contribute to violations of the annual PM2.5 NAAQS,
  requiring LDEQ to deny the Title V Permit renewal application."
- Yes: "II. No Piecemealing of Permits: The Army Corps Must Withdraw Permit MVN 2014-00702-CM
  and Analyze the Impacts of the Full Project Under All Applicable Statutes and Regulations."

Each heading names the actor, the act, and the legal consequence. Subheadings do the same one
level down, in the same grammatical form.

### The ask goes in paragraph one, numbered

Never bury the ask. State it before any background, in numbered form, so it can be quoted
back. From the Formosa letter:

> "Commenters wish to alert LDEQ to new air modeling results that require LDEQ to: 1.) deny
> Formosa Plastics' renewal application, or substantially reduce the complex's fine particulate
> (PM2.5) emissions, because they cause or contribute to National Ambient Air Quality Standards
> (NAAQS) violations; and 2.) act immediately to remedy broader, modeled NAAQS violations in
> St. James and surrounding parishes shown in this model, irrespective of Formosa Plastics'
> contributions."

Note the second ask: relief that extends beyond this applicant. Always consider whether the
evidence supports a demand the permit decision alone cannot satisfy. It costs one clause and it
preserves an issue against the agency's whole program.

### Reserve rights and signal what comes next

> "If LDEQ nonetheless intends to renew Formosa Plastics' Title V Permits without change,
> Commenters intend to submit more extensive comments during the required public comment
> period."

> "We request a public hearing in Cameron Parish, written notice of LDNR's decision, and we
> reserve the right to rely on all public comments submitted."

Three functions: preserves procedural rights, tells the agency this is not the last filing, and
puts the hearing request in the record where its denial becomes reviewable.

### Cc the supervisor

The Formosa letter to LDEQ copies three EPA Region 6 officials by name and email. The Shintech
petition went over LDEQ's head to the EPA Administrator entirely. A cc line is a costless
reminder that the state agency is not the last word — federal oversight, delegated-program
authority, a SIP call. Address the agency; copy the agency that can overrule it.

## SECTION 3: THE MOVES

Fourteen techniques, each with the mechanism and the source. Worked examples in
`references/excerpts.md`.

### Move 1 — Re-run the applicant's own model

**The single highest-value move in the corpus.** Do not dispute the applicant's data. Adopt it
completely — their emission inventory, their meteorological data, their software, the modeling
files they submitted to the agency — and change only the *scope* of the analysis.

Earthjustice retained a licensed PE with 44 years of Clean Air Act experience, who obtained
Formosa's own AERMOD files and re-ran them across a 50 km radius instead of the applicant's
narrow one. Result: an unbroken block of annual PM2.5 violations more than 17 miles long and 9
miles wide, reaching a named elementary school.

Why it is unanswerable: every objection the agency would normally raise — biased methodology,
non-standard model, cherry-picked inputs — was pre-emptively surrendered. The only remaining
explanation for the different result is the assumption that was changed, which is now the
agency's problem.

State this explicitly in the letter: *"Using [applicant]'s own emissions data and modeling
assumptions that the company provided to [agency]…"* The sentence is doing legal work.

### Move 2 — Attack the assumption, not the arithmetic

Applicants rarely make arithmetic errors. They make *input* choices. Find the input that was
selected rather than measured, and show that a better one was available and was not used.

The Formosa background-monitor argument is the model. Formosa did not conduct the year of
onsite monitoring the Clean Air Act presumptively requires (42 U.S.C. § 7475(e)); it borrowed a
"representative" background value of 7.6 µg/m³ from LDEQ's Geismar monitor — 14 miles upriver,
in a different parish. Meanwhile LDEQ's *own* in-parish monitor, 3 miles from the site, was
averaging 9.48 µg/m³ — already above the standard.

The structure: (1) the law presumes measurement; (2) the applicant substituted an assumption;
(3) the agency's own data contradicts the assumption; (4) therefore the results are
underestimates and the real violation area is larger. Every step is the agency's own record.

Inputs worth auditing every time: background/baseline values, receptor grid extent and density,
modeling radius, the emissions inventory of *other* sources, the meteorological year, whether
the current standard was used, and whether the analysis was ever updated after a rule changed.

### Move 3 — Catch the rule change the applicant slept through

Regulatory floors move. Applications take years. Check what changed between submission and
decision, and whether the applicant re-ran anything.

EPA lowered the annual PM2.5 NAAQS from 12.0 to 9.0 µg/m³ less than a month after Formosa filed.
Formosa did not update its modeling. LDEQ did not require it to. LDEQ approved anyway — and its
own approval letter conceded that a compliance demonstration against the new 9.0 standard would
be required at renewal. That concession then became the lever.

Always check: new or revised NAAQS, new MACT/NSPS, revised guidance, a new designation, and
anything the agency itself said in a prior approval letter about what it would require later.

### Move 4 — Close the escape hatch before it opens

Know the applicant's standard defense and dismantle it in the comment, not in the reply. The
recurring one in air permitting is the Significant Impact Level: *the area may be in violation,
but our contribution is below the SIL, so we are not responsible.*

The Formosa letter closes it twice over. First factually — Formosa exceeds the SIL for the
annual standard across an area comprising 17 percent of the total area modeled, so the defense
does not even apply on its own terms. Then legally, for the 24-hour standard where the
contribution *is* below the SIL: EPA's own SILs Guidance (at 18) states that sub-SIL
contributions may still "cause or contribute" where there is a "basis for concern," with
*Sierra Club v. EPA*, 705 F.3d 458, 464–65 (D.C. Cir. 2013) and *Powder River Basin Res. Council
v. Wyo. DEQ*, 226 P.3d 809, 818–19 (Wyo. 2010) behind it.

Note the belt-and-suspenders construction: *even if* the threshold defense were available, it
fails here; *and* it is not available. Build the argument so the agency must defeat both.

### Move 5 — Name the adverse precedent and distinguish it on new record evidence

This is the mark of a comment written by someone who expects to be in court. Do not omit the
case that hurts you — the agency will cite it, and having ignored it costs you credibility with
the reader who matters.

The Formosa letter concedes that the Louisiana First Circuit had recently affirmed LDEQ's
reliance on SILs in the original PSD permit, then distinguishes it on two independent grounds:
(1) that case was wrongly decided and certiorari is pending; and (2) — the real work — the First
Circuit relied on LDEQ's conclusion that violations would touch only industrial areas, not
residential ones, and *the new modeling shows the violations extend well into residential
areas*. The precedent is not attacked; its factual predicate is removed.

The pattern: **concede the holding → identify the factual premise it rested on → show your new
record evidence defeats that premise.** This is why Move 1 matters so much: new evidence is what
makes adverse precedent distinguishable.

### Move 6 — Anti-segmentation (piecemealing)

When a project arrives as an original permit plus a later "modification," check whether the
modification is actually essential to the original. If it is, the two were always one project
and the review was unlawfully split.

The SLM letter assembles it in four parts:

1. **The applicant's own admission.** SLM stated in its modification application that the
   project is "not viable" without the storage and transport components. That single word does
   most of the work.
2. **The legal standard.** Segmentation occurs where an agency "divides connected, cumulative,
   or similar federal actions into separate projects and thereby fails to address the true scope
   and impact of the activities" (*Kleppe v. Sierra Club*, 427 U.S. 390, 410 (1976)); a component
   without "substantial independent utility" cannot be severed (*Del. Riverkeeper Network v.
   FERC*, 753 F.3d 1304, 1313–17 (D.C. Cir. 2014)).
3. **The plain-language analogy** — see Move 7.
4. **The downstream collapse.** This is the part most drafters miss. Segmentation is not just a
   procedural foul; it invalidates the *substantive* tests that relied on the understated
   footprint. SLM chose its site because of "limited wetlands impacts" — but the true combined
   footprint is up to 38.9 acres, larger than alternatives it rejected. The site-selection
   rationale therefore fails on its own terms, and the §404(b)(1) practicable-alternatives
   analysis must be redone from scratch.

Always run step 4. Ask: which finding did the agency make in reliance on the understated scope,
and does that finding survive the corrected number?

### Move 7 — One plain-language analogy, precisely placed

The corpus contains almost no figurative language. Where it appears, it is a structural analogy
that makes a procedural violation legible, placed immediately after the legal standard:

> "SLM's actions are akin to applying for a permit to build an airport terminal and then years
> later asking for another permit to build a runway to serve the terminal next to a park, while
> knowing full well that the full project would not have been approved had it been revealed that
> the necessary runway would be next to a park."

Rules: one per document. It must clarify a *procedural* mechanism, never dramatize harm. It goes
after the citation, never before. It must be literally analogous — a reader should be able to
map every element onto the facts.

### Move 8 — Hold the agency to its own words

Regulations are the floor. Agencies also publish guidance documents, applicant handbooks, FAQ
pages, prior approval letters, and mission statements — and they are far more specific than the
regulations, which is exactly why they are useful.

Three forms in the corpus:

- **Applicant guidance.** The CP2 letter cites LDNR's own *Guide to Developing Alternatives and
  Justification Analyses* for what the agency has deemed sufficient: an alternatives analysis
  "should provide documentation that clearly demonstrates that reasonable efforts were made to
  find less damaging sites and should provide an explanation for why each less damaging site was
  not feasible." Venture Global's did not.
- **The agency's own characterization of the resource.** "As LDNR itself explained: 'Almost
  one-third of Louisiana's people live in the coastal area. For ecological, economic, and
  recreational reasons, this vast ecosystem is priceless. If lost, it cannot be replaced.'"
- **The agency's prior commitments.** LDEQ's PSD extension letter promised it would require a
  compliance demonstration against the new NAAQS at renewal. The comment holds it to that.

Then close the loop with the constitutional point: an agency errs by "assuming that its duty was
to adhere only to its own regulations rather than to the constitutional and statutory mandates"
(*Save Ourselves*, 452 So.2d at 1160). Its own guidance is evidence of what it knows adequacy
requires; the constitution is the reason regulatory minimum compliance is not a defense.

### Move 9 — Incompleteness as an independent, sufficient ground for denial

The most reusable argument in the whole corpus, because it does not require an expert.

The frame: the agency cannot make the findings the law requires it to make, because the
applicant did not supply the information those findings require. Granting on this record is
therefore premature and arbitrary or capricious — *regardless of the merits*. And separately,
the missing information deprived the public of a meaningful opportunity to comment.

Execution, from CP2: a bulleted list of specific omissions, each tied to the Coastal Use
Guideline it defeats — no alternative-site wetland acreages, no pipeline routes for the
alternatives, no basis for the proposed capacity versus a smaller one, no quantification of
costs and benefits, no cumulative-impacts assessment.

Procedural tells worth hunting, all from the corpus:

- The application has been in "on hold" status for months — the agency's own website says the
  record is incomplete.
- Supplemental information was filed in a *related docket only* and never opened for comment in
  this one.
- The application was published to EDMS the same day it was granted.
- Approval issued with no public notice at all.
- The applicant cites a pending federal review that has not concluded.

Each of these is a dated, documentary fact. Cite the docket number and the date.

### Move 10 — Incorporation by reference

Put thousands of pages of prior technical and legal material into this administrative record
without lengthening this document:

> "…which are attached to, hereby incorporated fully into, and further supplemented by these
> Comments."

> "This letter is incorporated by reference, and we urge you to refer to it for more detail."

Use for: your own earlier comments in a related docket, expert reports, coalition partners'
submissions, and — where the rules permit — all public comments filed by others. Name the
document, its date, its docket, and attach it. The agency must now review the whole body of
material, and every issue in it is preserved.

### Move 11 — Praise, then demand parity

Where the decision-maker has previously done the right thing on an analogous matter, open by
acknowledging it specifically and by name, quote their own words back, and then demand the same
treatment here. The SLM letter opens by thanking the Acting Assistant Secretary for ordering a
full EIS on Formosa Plastics, quotes his memorandum ("The EIS process will provide opportunities
for voices to be heard in an open, transparent, and public way"), and closes by returning to it:

> "As you showed leadership centering environmental justice with the Formosa Plastics 404
> permit, we ask that you show the same leadership here…"

It reframes the ask as consistency rather than concession, and it makes refusal require an
explanation of why these facts are different. Open with it and return to it in the conclusion.

### Move 12 — Refuse the applicant's evidence about itself

An applicant's safety, hydrology, and compliance representations are assertions about its own
future conduct, usually supported by studies it commissioned. Name the source of the claim, then
state the absence of independent support in one sentence:

> "Yet SLM claims—citing only the company's own study—that these massive, daily withdrawals
> 'will have no adverse impacts,' and even that all of this pumping will improve the aquifer
> because it 'will induce freshwater from the [Mississippi] to infiltrate the Gramercy.' There
> is no evidence for these claims."

The construction is worth copying exactly: attribute (*citing only the company's own study*),
quote the claim so its implausibility is visible without commentary, and close flat. No adjective
does any work in that passage.

Pair it with a baseline the agency can check. SLM's 13 MGD withdrawal means nothing standing
alone; against the USGS figure that the entire parish withdraws 2.4 MGD from that aquifer, it
argues itself.

**The stronger version, where it is available:** if the applicant or an affiliate already
operates a comparable facility, its own operating record is the observed data against which its
predictions can be tested — semi-annual monitoring reports, Title V deviation reports,
enforcement history, spill reports. A safety claim contradicted by the applicant's own reports on
a sister facility is very difficult for an agency to credit. *(This technique is documented in
supplemental CP2 filings that are not among the four source letters excerpted here, so no
verbatim model is provided for it.)* Use `bayou:epa-echo-search` for compliance and enforcement
history, `bayou:epa-campd-search` for unit-level excess emissions, `bayou:csb-nrc-hazmat` for
incident and release reports, and `bayou:osha-inspections` for citations.

### Move 13 — Lived experience enters as record evidence tied to a statutory factor

This is where community voice belongs, and the rule is strict: **it enters as evidence bearing
on a factor the agency must weigh, with a citation, not as testimony to how bad things are.**

Three verified forms:

- **A documented petition as evidence of public opposition** — a public-interest factor. SLM
  cites a December 2013 petition of District 5 residents to LDEQ "providing hundreds of
  signatures protesting the location," with the EDMS document link.
- **Expert affidavit converting a community concern into a technical finding.** Residents raised
  "the possibility of a leak, fire or explosion." Dr. Petra Pless, an environmental engineer and
  safety specialist, then found methanol storage incompatible with adjacent recreational and
  residential uses and catalogued comparable catastrophic incidents. The concern is now a
  land-use incompatibility finding the agency must address.
- **Named, specific receptors instead of general population.** Not "nearby communities" — "the
  downriver community of Welcome, such as the St. Louis Academy elementary school"; "adjacent to
  Welcome Park, which is a large public complex that includes a walking path, playground
  equipment, ball field… and community meeting hall for District 5." Specific named places
  inside a modeled violation contour are what make an impact finding unavoidable.

And one rhetorical beat per document, at most. In 62 pages, the CP2 letter spends exactly one:

> "There must be a point when LDNR's Office of Coastal Management says, 'Enough.'"

It lands because everything around it is flat. Spend it in the conclusion of a section, never in
the opening, and never twice.

### Move 14 — Demographics with a citable source, and pre-empt the confounder defense

State the disproportionate burden as sourced numbers with a comparison baseline, then close the
predictable counterargument before it is made.

Sourced comparison (SLM, footnote, citing Census QuickFacts): "The 5th District of St. James
Parish is 93% Black, St. James parish overall is only 49% Black, and the state of Louisiana is
just 32.8% Black."

Marginal-contribution math (Shintech): Shintech's 104,520 lb/yr of vinyl chloride would be a 75%
increase over *total statewide* releases — "Yet the entire 104,520 pounds of vinyl chloride
emissions will not be spread out throughout the state: they will be confined to St. James
Parish." Total-versus-local framing turns one facility's number into a distributional fact.

Confounder pre-emption (SLM, quoting Terrell et al. 2021): the researchers "revealed no evidence
to suggest that these lifestyle factors were responsible for the putative link between pollution
and cancer rates." Whenever you cite a health-disparity study, quote the passage where the
authors addressed smoking, obesity, or access to care. The agency's stock response is that
lifestyle explains it; answer it in the footnote where it is raised.

Also available: the agency's failure to conduct any EJ review at all is its own defect —
"The Applicant fails to provide any information to enable LDNR to undertake an
environmental-justice review" — which folds into Move 9.

## SECTION 4: CITATION DISCIPLINE

Every factual assertion carries a citation to a record document or a public source. There are no
exceptions and no floating facts.

- **Record documents:** name, document number, page, date. "PSD Application, EDMS Doc. No.
  14215546, at p. 240 of 588." "Permit Modification Notice at 1." Pin cites, always.
- **The applicant's own words:** quote them, and quote enough that the context cannot be
  disputed. An applicant's admission cited to its own page number is the most durable evidence
  in the letter.
- **Cases:** full cite, pin cite, and a parenthetical stating the proposition — "(holding
  contributions less than SIL to NAAQS violations could violate the Act)."
- **Agency guidance:** title, date, page, and a live URL.
- **Science:** author, title, journal or institution, year, page, URL.
- **Never characterize an authority from memory.** Verify every Louisiana citation against
  `skills/public-comment/references/louisiana-hooks.md`, which carries status and known traps,
  and use `bayou:la-rs-search` and `bayou:lac33-search` for statutes and LAC provisions. If a
  case cannot be verified, cut it — one miscited case is used to discredit the entire filing.
- **NEPA authorities need re-verification before use.** The segmentation and cumulative-impacts
  case law in the source letters predates significant statutory and judicial change to NEPA's
  scope. The technique transfers; confirm the current standard before citing the case.

Substantive footnotes are good practice and used heavily in the corpus — put the confounder
rebuttal, the procedural objection, the alternative reading, and the "we continue to object"
preservation language in footnotes so the body stays readable.

## SECTION 5: COMPOSING WITH `bayou:public-comment`

`bayou:public-comment` produces a filed letter with its own output spec: a standing or interest
statement, numbered comments each ending in the posture's endpoint (a draftable permit condition,
a rule-text edit, or a record disclosure), an enumerated relief request, and a candor section.
This style's corpus is permit-posture work. Under the rulemaking or financing posture, take the
moves that transfer, such as holding the agency to its own words and incompleteness as a ground,
and leave the permit-specific ones. This style is compatible with that spec and does not replace it.

**Where they differ, `public-comment` wins on voice.** That skill writes an individual
commenter's letter: first person singular, plain, contractions allowed. The corpus behind *this*
skill is institutional counsel writing for organizations. When drafting under `public-comment`,
take the *structural* moves from this file — headings that state the demand, the ask in ¶1,
incompleteness as a ground, incorporation by reference, adverse-precedent distinction, citation
discipline — and leave the institutional register behind.

Three things carry across unchanged in every context: **no adjective carries an argument; every
factual assertion is cited; the candor section is never softened.**

## SECTION 6: SELF-CHECK

Run this against the finished draft. Each line is pass/fail. Fix every failure or state why not.

**Structure**
1. The ask appears in paragraph one, numbered, before any background.
2. Every substantive heading is a full sentence naming the actor, the act, and the legal
   consequence — reading only the headings conveys the whole case.
3. The document ends in an enumerated relief request, and the last item closes forward (what
   the agency must conclude once it does the analysis).
4. Rights are reserved and any hearing request is on the record.
5. There is a cc line to whoever supervises the addressee, where one exists.

**Substance**
6. Every numbered argument ends in a draftable permit condition, a specific record demand, or
   denial.
7. Every factual assertion carries a citation to a record document or public source, with a pin
   cite.
8. At least one argument would independently require denial or remand if the agency conceded
   nothing else.
9. The strongest contrary authority or fact is named and distinguished, not omitted.
10. Where the applicant's data was disputed, the applicant's own inputs were used and only the
    scope or assumption was changed.
11. Each finding the agency made in reliance on a number you corrected has been re-examined.
12. Community evidence is tied to a statutory factor and cited to a document.

**Register**
13. No adjective is carrying an argument — every intensifier has been replaced by the number
    that justified it.
14. No metaphor, no sensory language, no characterization of motive.
15. At most one rhetorical beat and at most one analogy in the entire document.
16. "Must" is used wherever the law supports it; "should" appears only for genuinely
    discretionary asks.
17. No hedges — "we feel," "we are concerned that," "arguably," "it seems."
18. Every authority has been verified against a source, not recalled from memory.

**Institutional drafting only** — skip these two when the letter is an individual commenter's,
including anything drafted under `bayou:public-comment`, whose voice spec allows first person
singular and contractions:

19. No contractions.
